# The Image Never Left the Sentence

## From cave walls to AI-generated visual prose

A sentence today is almost blind.

It can name a hand axe, a Neanderthal skull, a temple, a cell, or a distant species with extraordinary precision. But the thing itself remains elsewhere: in another tab, below a figure number, behind a citation, or inside the reader's imagination. Modern writing is excellent at carrying language. It is less willing to carry the visible world alongside it.

This was not always the dominant relationship between seeing and reading.

Long before the page, people used stone walls, bone, ivory, pigment, and repeated marks to make thought persist outside the body. Later writing systems did not simply replace pictures with words. They learned to make one sign perform several jobs at once: resemble a thing, name a thing, suggest a sound, classify a word, mark a number, or orient a reader within a larger scene.

**Neanderthal** begins from a simple proposal: what if AI-generated text could recover some of that partnership between word and image—not by turning language back into pictograms, but by placing small, inspectable visual capsules exactly where understanding needs them, directly on the typographic baseline?

---

## Before writing, the wall was an interface

More than 57,000 years ago, Neanderthals traced deliberate, spatially organized marks into the walls of La Roche-Cotard cave in France ![La Roche-Cotard Neanderthal finger tracings](neanderthal:image). Archaeologists describe them as the oldest unambiguous Neanderthal cave-wall engravings currently known. The marks are non-figurative, and we cannot recover what they meant, but evidence from the sealed cave shows that they were made intentionally rather than produced by tools, animals, or geology. They are proof of *designed marks*, not proof of writing. ([Marquet et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0286568))

On the Indonesian island of Sulawesi, a painted scene of human-like figures interacting with a pig ![Leang Karampuang cave painting](neanderthal:image) has been dated to at least 51,200 years ago. Its figures are composed so that a viewer can infer an action. The researchers describe it as the earliest known surviving example of representational art and visual storytelling. A story could now remain on a surface after its maker had left. ([Oktaviana et al., *Nature*, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07541-7))

Other prehistoric objects carry repeated lines, dots, crosses, grids, and notches. A 2026 study of more than 200 Aurignacian objects from Central Europe argued that some 40,000-year-old sign sequences had statistical properties comparable to early proto-cuneiform and may have formed a conventional information system. That interpretation is already being challenged: critics point out that statistical regularity alone cannot prove shared meaning, linguistic content, or communicative purpose. ([Bentz and Dutkiewicz, *PNAS*, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2520385123); [d'Errico et al., preprint, 2026](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7053279))

That disagreement matters. A cave painting is not automatically a script, just as a photograph is not automatically a sentence. Writing requires a community to share conventions that connect visible marks to language. The honest historical story is therefore not a straight evolutionary arrow from cave painting to alphabet. It is a much longer experiment in **externalizing memory**: giving an event, object, count, identity, or idea a durable visual form.

The cave wall was not a primitive book page. It was closer to a shared screen whose marks could summon a story, memory, ritual, route, animal, or social identity in the presence of people who knew how to interpret it.

## When pictures learned to speak

The decisive change was not that people became better at drawing. It was that marks became standardized enough to be read by people other than their maker—and flexible enough to represent language.

In southern Mesopotamia, proto-cuneiform appeared near the end of the fourth millennium BCE. Early signs were drawn into wet clay as pictographs and abstract marks, often for administration: goods, labor, land, quantities, and transactions. Over time, the curved drawing gave way to combinations of wedge-shaped impressions ![Proto-cuneiform clay tablet](neanderthal:image), better suited to a reed stylus and faster repetition. The image was not merely simplified by taste; it was transformed by the material, the tool, and the expanding work demanded of writing. ([Metropolitan Museum of Art, “The Origins of Writing”](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-origins-of-writing))

Egyptian hieroglyphs made the cooperation between image and language especially visible. A hieroglyphic inscription was never just a row of tiny drawings whose meanings could be guessed from appearance. Its signs could represent consonantal sounds, complete words, or semantic categories. A picture placed at the end of a word could act as a **determinative**: it was normally silent, but it told the reader what kind of thing the word referred to—a person, settlement, action, substance, or idea. ([UCL Digital Egypt, “The System of Egyptian Hieroglyphic Writing”](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/writing/system.html); [British Museum, “Finding the Key to Ancient Egypt”](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/eureka-finding-key-ancient-egypt))

This is remarkably close to a visual annotation embedded in language. The word carries the sound; the image narrows the field of meaning.

Egyptian writing also resisted the rigid geometry of the modern baseline. Scribes balanced signs inside imaginary rectangular groups, now called quadrats ![Egyptian hieroglyphic quadrats](neanderthal:image), and could arrange inscriptions horizontally or vertically. Humans, animals, and birds faced toward the beginning of a line; signs associated with a depicted person generally shared that person's orientation. In monumental art, writing and image could be so tightly fused that drawing a clean border between them becomes difficult. ([Unicode Standard 16.0, Chapter 11](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-11/); [UCL Digital Egypt, “Art and Script in Ancient Egypt”](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/art/artscript.html))

The Maya developed another mixed system. Their hieroglyphs combined whole-word signs with syllabic signs and appeared on stone monuments, ceramics, and folded books ![Maya hieroglyphic codex](neanderthal:image). A glyph was at once a designed visual block and a unit of readable language. ([Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, “Reading the Calendar Glyphs”](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/calendar/reading-calendar-glyphs))

Chinese writing is often described too casually as pictorial. Some characters did begin as stylized depictions, but the mature system is not a gallery of universally guessable pictures. Most characters depend on convention, and many combine a component that hints at meaning with another that hints at sound. This is another version of the same deep pattern: writing becomes powerful when **appearance, meaning, and sound cooperate rather than compete**. ([Branner, “China: Writing System,” *Encyclopedia of Language & Linguistics*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/chinese-character))

The great invention of early scripts was therefore not the pictogram alone. It was the hybrid sign.

## The alphabet did not destroy the image. It compressed it.

Alphabetic writing solved a different problem. Instead of requiring hundreds of word signs, a small reusable set of signs could represent units of speech. Greek writers adapted the Phoenician alphabet; the Latin alphabet later descended from a western Greek form through Etruscan. ([Unicode Standard 16.0, Chapter 7](https://www.unicode.org/versions/Unicode16.0.0/core-spec/chapter-7/))

This was an extraordinary compression technology. With a few dozen repeatable marks, people could write unfamiliar names, arguments, laws, poems, jokes, and abstractions. The alphabet's strength is precisely that a letter does not need to resemble what a word means. The shapes in *bird* do not need feathers.

So the disappearance of pictures from ordinary alphabetic prose should not be told as a simple story of decline. Even ancient Egyptian writing developed faster cursive forms for letters, accounts, and other everyday tasks; the writing tool and the need for speed made many signs less visibly pictorial. ([UCL Digital Egypt, “The Hieratic Script”](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/museums-static/digitalegypt/writing/hieratic.html))

What changed was the division of labor.

The alphabet carried pronunciation. Words carried description. Images became illustrations, plates, diagrams, maps, figures, and later thumbnails—usually positioned beside or beneath the main text rather than inside its rhythm. Visual symbols survived in specialist islands: mathematical notation, currency signs, musical notation, astronomical symbols, maps, road signs, interface icons, and brand marks. They did not disappear; they became a parallel layer that prose occasionally points toward.

That is the sense in which modern writing has “forgotten” the image. This is a design argument, not a claim that history moved backward. The modern page can contain both text and pictures, but its default sentence asks the reader to imagine every referent or leave the sentence to see it.

A citation proves that a door exists. It does not open the door inside the sentence.

## Emoji are a return of pictorial feeling—not pictorial knowledge

Emoji brought colorful pictographs back into everyday text. Unicode defines emoji as pictographs that can be used inline, and their position inside a message lets them behave like tone, gesture, reaction, emphasis, object, or punctuation. ([Unicode Emoji Technical Report](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr51/))

But emoji are not a modern replacement for hieroglyphic writing. They rarely encode the grammar or full vocabulary of a language. Their power often comes from productive ambiguity: the same symbol can soften a request, signal irony, mark approval, or change meaning across communities. Even the visual rendering can shift across platforms enough to change how a message's sentiment is interpreted. ([Miller et al., GroupLens Research, 2016](https://grouplens.org/blog/investigating-the-potential-for-miscommunication-using-emoji/))

Emoji help us *feel* the sentence. Citations help us *verify* the sentence. Conventional illustrations help us *see around* the sentence.

An inline visual capsule should do something different: help us **understand the exact subject at the moment we encounter it**.

## A visual capsule is not decoration

Consider this sentence:

> Neanderthals shaped Mousterian stone tools to create sharp flakes for cutting and scraping.

For a reader who has never seen one, “Mousterian stone tool” is an empty verbal container. A full-width museum photograph placed three paragraphs later may help, but it breaks the reading flow and forces the reader to reconnect image and phrase.

Now place the referent on the line:

> Neanderthals shaped Mousterian stone tools ![Mousterian stone tools](neanderthal:image) to create sharp flakes for cutting and scraping.

The capsule is small enough to preserve the sentence but distinct enough to become a visual foothold. A glance answers *what kind of thing is this?* Hovering can reveal the title and source. Opening it can expose the high-resolution image, caption, date, creator, and provenance.

This interaction follows a useful learning principle: corresponding words and pictures are generally easier to integrate when they are presented near one another rather than separated across a page or screen. In multimedia-learning research, this is called the **spatial contiguity principle**. ([Mayer, *Multimedia Learning*, Cambridge University Press](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9781139164603.008))

The typographic baseline is therefore more than a visual trick. It is an address. It binds a visual referent to the phrase that gives it meaning.

The capsule also acts like a museum label folded into a pebble: small while the reader is moving, detailed when curiosity asks for more.

## Where AI-generated answers benefit most

AI produces language cheaply and at enormous speed. That makes a new problem visible: an answer can sound fluent while asking the reader to mentally construct dozens of unfamiliar things. Inline visual capsules are most useful when they reduce that construction work without interrupting the explanation.

### 1. Concrete but unfamiliar entities

History, archaeology, biology, astronomy, architecture, art, and engineering are filled with names that do not form an image for a beginner: an Acheulean hand axe ![Acheulean hand axe](neanderthal:image), a cuneiform tablet, a tardigrade, a flying buttress, a mitochondrion, or a specific instrument. A sourced image gives the noun an immediate visual referent.

### 2. Mechanisms and causal explanations

When an answer explains *how something works*, a well-chosen diagram can show relationships that prose must describe serially: the path of blood through the heart, the stages of an eclipse, the layers of a neural network, or the motion of a geological fault. The image should appear at the causal step it explains, not in a detached gallery.

### 3. Spatial knowledge

Maps, anatomy, interfaces, artifacts, and physical systems depend on position. “Below,” “inside,” “adjacent,” and “through” become easier to understand when the relevant visual is attached to the phrase that establishes the relationship.

### 4. Comparisons that language makes expensive

AI often answers questions such as “How are these two species different?” or “How did this tool evolve?” Carefully selected paired visuals can reveal proportion, silhouette, material, and structure faster than a paragraph of adjectives. The prose should still state the conclusion; the visual supplies inspectable evidence.

### 5. Vocabulary and language learning

Inline pictures can operate as visual glosses for concrete words. Evidence is not uniformly positive for every vocabulary task, but a recent meta-analysis found that, on immediate tests, combining first-language words with pictures outperformed either words or pictures alone in second-language vocabulary learning; the delayed advantage was less certain. The important condition is that the picture clarifies the target rather than merely decorating the page. ([Zhang, Sala, and Gobet, *Educational Research Review*, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100681))

### 6. Claims anchored in primary objects

For art history, archaeology, material culture, and natural science, the image can be part of the evidence itself. A capsule can connect a claim to a manuscript fragment, specimen, excavation object, satellite image, or museum record. This is where provenance matters most: the reader should be able to move from thumbnail to source without trusting the AI blindly.

The design is less useful for purely abstract transitions, familiar objects, or claims whose truth cannot be shown visually. Adding a capsule to every noun would turn reading into visual static.

## Why it may amplify learning

The claim should remain precise: pictures do not automatically make people learn, and no interface can manufacture understanding through decoration alone.

There are, however, several reasons to expect carefully selected inline visuals to help:

- **Two representations:** words and relevant pictures can give the learner verbal and pictorial ways to organize the same concept.
- **Immediate grounding:** the reader does not need to postpone an unfamiliar term or search for it elsewhere.
- **Stronger retrieval cues:** experiments have found familiar graphic symbols to be better remembered than their word counterparts, although later work also shows that an unfamiliar symbol without known meaning loses that advantage. ([Roberts, MacLeod, and Fernandes, *Cognition*, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105435); [Roberts, Tran, and Fernandes, *Scientific Reports*, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-87612-5))
- **Progressive disclosure:** the reader can glance, hover, or inspect according to need instead of absorbing every detail at once.
- **Curiosity without departure:** the visual can invite exploration while keeping the reader inside the conceptual flow.

Direct research on AI-composed, inline visual explanations is still young. One small 2025 study of an AI-augmented digital textbook reported improved post-reading scores when generated summaries and images were added, but its sample of 24 participants is far too small to settle the question. It is a promising signal, not proof of a general effect. ([Morita et al., “GenAIReading,” 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.07463))

The stronger evidence comes from the broader science of multimedia learning—and it includes a warning. Interesting but irrelevant images can distract attention and slightly reduce comprehension. In other words, a beautiful visual that does not explain the nearby idea can make the lesson worse. ([González, Saux, and Burin, *Australasian Journal of Educational Technology*, 2019](https://doi.org/10.14742/ajet.4577))

The ambition is not “more images.” It is **less distance between an idea and the evidence needed to see it**.

## A design constitution for visual prose

If inline capsules are to become a learning medium rather than a novelty, they need rules.

### Every visual must earn its position

Insert a capsule only when it identifies an unfamiliar entity, explains a relationship, supports a comparison, or exposes evidence. If removing the image changes nothing, the image was decoration.

### Attach the image to the smallest meaningful phrase

The capsule belongs immediately after the words it clarifies. This relationship should remain stable while an AI answer streams so that the reader's visual map of the paragraph does not keep shifting.

### Prefer evidence over synthetic plausibility

For factual subjects, a real specimen, object, document, location, or vetted diagram is usually more valuable than an AI-generated approximation. Synthetic images should be clearly labeled and should never masquerade as historical or scientific evidence.

### Reveal provenance at the same speed as curiosity

The first view can be compact. Hover or focus should reveal a title and origin. Inspection should expose the source page, creator, date, license when available, and a larger view. Fluency should never hide uncertainty.

### Preserve the sentence

The capsule should align with the type, respect line height, avoid violent reflow, and remain subordinate to reading. It is a visual clause, not an advertisement inserted between words.

### Design for more than sight

Every informative image needs an equivalent text alternative, and complex diagrams need a fuller description. Keyboard access, visible focus, reduced motion, and nonvisual source access are part of the medium—not optional repairs. ([W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, “Images Tutorial”](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/))

### Make absence honest

If no trustworthy visual can be found, show that state plainly. A missing image is better than a confident mismatch.

### Use density as an editorial decision

Some paragraphs need no image. Some need one anchor. A step-by-step mechanism may need several. The correct density follows the reader's conceptual load, not an engagement quota.

## Not a return to hieroglyphs

Neanderthal is not an attempt to replace the Latin alphabet with pictures. Pictures alone are too ambiguous for the full work of language, and writing systems became powerful precisely by escaping literal resemblance.

The project is closer to a reunion.

Words retain what they do best: sequence, grammar, abstraction, qualification, and argument. Images contribute what they do best: recognition, structure, proportion, material presence, and visual memory. Interaction controls the depth. Provenance connects the generated explanation to the world beyond the model.

Ancient scripts often made seeing and reading part of the same act. Modern digital text has the technical ability to do this again without giving up the alphabet's precision.

The cave wall held an image that waited for a storyteller. The hieroglyph made image and language share a surface. The typographic page usually placed illustration beside the sentence. Emoji returned small pictures to the line, mostly to carry feeling.

AI creates the opportunity for the next step: **visuals that appear at the point of meaning, remain attached to the language that explains them, and open into evidence when the reader asks.**

That is not a more decorative way to write.

It is a more visible way to think.
